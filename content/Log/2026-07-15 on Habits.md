- **Speech Habits** — Voice is something we can pay more attention to. 漏口 / 語言節律障礙.
	- An Instagram account teaches speech at 82 bpm. I find this reductive — words-per-minute only captures the surface of rhythm. It ignores the connections and relationships between words, and doesn't even work as a warm-up exercise.
	- To me, lengthening the pronunciation of each word is more meaningful. It forces you to listen into what you're saying.
	- Editing advantage: speaking slower and longer means that speeding up produces a natural — and still clear — pace.
	- Some people think clearer when they speak more; speaking allows them to articulate ideas in real time, and it's reasonable to circle back to clarify. I understand the value of this, especially for those who find expression difficult or hesitant.
	- However, this approach places a higher burden on the listener. If you can finish an idea in one sentence, why use ten?
	- I value brevity. In studying Chinese in Hong Kong, concise expression was also something to aspire to. 在香港學習中文，我們也崇尚精煉的用字。
	- A painter once said: 我從不浪費別人的時間。— "I never waste other people's time." That resonates.
	- Being concise is, of course, demanding for the speaker. In presentations, job interviews, or thesis defences, the speaker must be well-prepared because the listener is often a gatekeeper. In conversation, however, the dynamic is more fluid and negotiable — and so the appropriate style of speech is too. [[Speech and Sound]] — topic for another time.

> [!comment] Audience Note
> This is the richest section in the note and could easily become its own essay. The critique of the 82-bpm approach is sharp and shows genuine thinking about what rhythm actually means in speech. "It forces you to listen into what you're saying" is an excellent line — it captures something about the self-monitoring quality of slow speech that most people miss. The distinction between gatekeeper contexts (interviews, defences) and conversational contexts is a useful structural insight. The painter's quote lands well as a closer — it embodies the very principle being discussed. "Aritist" should be "artist."

## What Would a Good Working Day Look Like?

| Time         | Activity                                        |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Before sleep | Defrost breakfast, prepare clothes              |
| 00:00–07:30  | Sleep (air con off around 07:00, temperature up) |
| 07:30–08:30  | Brisk wake-up, stretch                          |
| 08:30–11:00  | **Session A**                                   |
| 11:00–12:00  | Breakfast and coffee                            |
| 14:30–17:30  | **Session B**                                   |
| 17:30–18:00  | Buffer                                          |
| 18:00–19:00  | Dinner                                          |
| 19:00–20:00  | Relax (walk / TV / scrolling)                   |
| 20:00–22:00  | **Session C**                                   |
| 22:30–23:00  | Shower                                          |
| 23:00–00:00  | Wind down — self-care and pre-sleep routine     |

> [!comment] Audience Note
> The structure is solid and the three-session model (A/B/C) gives good shape to the day. A few observations: there's a notable gap between Session A ending at 11:00 and Session B starting at 14:30 — that's 3.5 hours, which is a long buffer. Intentional? Also, the schedule has no explicit entry for lunch. If the 16:8 fasting window is in play, that might explain the gap. The pre-sleep routine starting at 23:00 aligns well with the midnight sleep target.
